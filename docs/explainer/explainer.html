Shortest Complete Palindrome

Palindromes · integer programming

The shortest palindrome that says everything it can

Take Peter Norvig’s dictionary of noun phrases. Exactly 64,811 of its phrases can appear in a palindromic sentence at all. The shortest sentence that contains every one of them has 814,402 or 814,403 letters, and a sentence of 814,403 is in hand.

The puzzle

“A man, a plan, a canal: Panama” reads the same in both directions once you drop the spaces and punctuation. In 2002 Peter Norvig asked a program to stretch it: keep the opening and the ending, and insert as many different noun phrases as possible in between, each used once. His dictionary, npdict.txt, has 126,342 entries drawn from the Moby word list. His first palindrome ran to 15,319 words, and the 2016 version reaches 21,012 words and 16,111 phrases.

In 2018 Roland Meertens changed the rules. Let phrases repeat, and ask how many different phrases one sentence can hold. He called such a sentence complete. He built a small palindromic sub-sentence around each dictionary phrase by depth-first search and joined them, reaching 64,810 distinct phrases in 954,401 placements, and wrote that it is safe to assume no more can be included.

That leaves two questions with exact answers:

  • How many phrases can be included? Exactly 64,811 of the 125,512 distinct ones. Meertens’s sentence has all but one of them, “M2M”.
  • How short can a complete sentence be? Meertens’s has 4,836,010 letters. The answer is 814,402 or 814,403, about a sixth of that.

A phrase counts by its letters and digits only, lowercased, as in Norvig’s and Meertens’s code: “a man” is aman. Lengths below are in those characters.

Build it from the outside in

A palindrome is easiest to grow from both ends at once. Put a phrase at the left end. Its letters now need a mirror image at the right end, so put a phrase there that supplies it. That phrase usually overshoots and leaves letters of its own unmatched, which the left side must answer next. Always add to the side that is behind.

The leftover is the whole state of the construction. There are three kinds:

  • E: the two sides are level.
  • (L, s): the left is ahead by s, the end of the last phrase placed there.
  • (R, p): the right is ahead by p, the start of the last phrase placed there.

A sentence may stop whenever the leftover is itself a palindrome, because the leftover is then the middle of the sentence. Such a state is called accepting.

A graph of leftovers

Make every possible leftover a node and draw an arc for every way of placing one phrase. Each arc carries the phrase it places.

Every palindromic sentence is exactly one walk in this graph from E to an accepting state, and every such walk is a sentence. For Norvig’s dictionary the graph has 716,216 states and 1,263,316 arcs.

The first question is now a question about reachability. A phrase can be included when it labels an arc that some walk from E to an accepting state uses. Of the states, 135,455 can still reach an accepting one, and the arcs among them carry 64,811 different phrases.

One sentence can hold all of them at once. If T is a palindromic sentence then so is TT, the sentence said twice, and TT is level in the middle. So every includable phrase lies on a closed walk that leaves E and returns to it, and closed walks through E can be nested one inside another. The computation confirms it directly: the 135,455 useful states form a single strongly connected component.

How short? A postman problem

A complete sentence is a walk that uses every includable phrase at least once, and its length is the sum of the letters on its arcs. Finding the shortest one is a directed rural postman problem: cover a required set of streets with the shortest closed route. Norvig’s recent Portmantout notebook solves a problem of the same family, the shortest string that contains every word of a list, and there the linear program lands on a whole-number answer by itself. Here it does not, for one reason: a phrase labels many arcs, one for each state it can be played from, and the walk needs only one of them.

Count how often the walk uses each arc. Let xa be that count for arc a, let w(a) be the phrase on the arc and ℓw its length. A walk that ends in an accepting state f is closed up by a free return arc to E, counted by rf; F is the set of accepting states.

The first family of constraints says the walk leaves every state as often as it enters it. The second says every phrase is used. The third allows one return to E, so the flow describes a single sentence. One requirement is left out: that the arcs in use hang together as one walk. It turns out not to matter here, since every flow found was already in one piece, and a flow in pieces can be joined through E at a small cost.

For this dictionary the model has 135,455 states, 426,676 arcs, 64,811 phrases and 903 return arcs. A whole-number flow is turned back into a sentence by Hierholzer’s algorithm for Eulerian circuits, and the sentence is then checked from scratch: reverse the string, look up every phrase, count the distinct ones.

Half a palindrome, and the cut that forbids it

Drop the whole-number requirement and the model is a linear program. Its optimum is 813,105.25, so no complete sentence has fewer than 813,106 letters. The best sentence built by rounding that solution has 815,407. The gap comes from flows of one half.

The remedy is a family of inequalities that every whole-number flow satisfies and the half-unit loops violate. Take any set S of states and any set W of phrases with an odd number of members. Write AW for the arcs labelled by a phrase in W, and δ−(S) and δ+(S) for the arcs entering and leaving S. Add the covering constraints of W to the balance of flow across the boundary of S, and halve:

12∑w∈W∑a:w(a)=wxa+12(x(δ−(S))−x(δ+(S)))≥|W|2

Arcs of W that enter S now have coefficient 1, those that leave have 0, and the other arcs of W and the other entering arcs have one half. Round the halves up, drop the negative terms, and the left side is a whole number for any whole-number flow. The right side, W being odd, is not, so it rounds up too:

x(AW∖δ+(S))+x(δ−(S)∖AW)≥|W|+12

In words: if every phrase of an odd set were covered exactly once by flow crossing the boundary of S, and nothing else crossed, an odd amount would cross, although as much must enter as leave. For “ababa”, S is the one state (L, ababa) and W is the phrase alone, and the cut demands a whole unit where the linear program had a half.

Finding violated cuts is its own search problem. A heuristic based on linear algebra over the two-element field found 1,460 of them and raised the bound to 814,371. Separating the same family exactly, as a small integer program, added 431 more and reached 814,391. There the family runs out: what stays fractional is one tangle of about 460 short bridging phrases, with flows at thirds and odder values.

Closing the gap

Two numbers move toward each other. A lower bound says no complete sentence can be shorter. An upper bound is the length of a sentence actually built and checked. The answer lies between the best of each.

The upper side moved in three ways. Rounding fixes one arc per phrase a few hundred phrases at a time and re-solves; once every phrase has its arc, what remains is an ordinary network flow, whose solutions are whole numbers. Starting that from the cut-tightened program instead of the plain one took a sentence from 815,407 letters to 814,503. Pinning the 64,479 phrases that the program already places on a single arc and running branch and bound on the other 332 gave 814,455.

The last 52 letters came from a commercial solver. Gurobi, run on the public NEOS server, improved the sentence at the root of its search to 814,419 and then to 814,403 by its own heuristics. It could not search further in the 3 GB of memory NEOS allows. Its cutting planes also lift the lower bound from 814,391 to 814,401.5, which rounds up to 814,402, the same figure the open-source solver HiGHS reaches.

How firm are the two ends? The sentence of 814,403 letters is checked independently of any solver. The lower bounds of 813,106 and 814,391 rest on a linear program plus cuts with a short proof. The final step to 814,402 rests on cuts generated inside two solvers that agree, and has not been verified separately.

The sentence

The best sentence has 153,670 phrase placements for its 64,811 distinct phrases. Said once each, the phrases would take 488,137 letters. The other 326,266 letters are glue: short phrases repeated to make the mirror images line up.

It begins and ends like this:

a supersensitiveness, a supersensitiser, Bal, a clientage, Gar, a civ, a parrot, Aili, CNO, … 153,652 more … Nes, Rep, USA, SSE, nevi, tis, Nes, Rep, USA

and its single middle letter is the second e of “reremmice”, a kind of bat.

What is settled and what is open

Letters in the shortest complete sentence814,402 or 814,403One letter open

Deciding the last letter means either finding a complete sentence of 814,402 letters or proving none exists. Both need a solver that can search the full model past its first node, which takes more memory than the free server offers and more speed than the open-source solvers have shown on this problem.
